Following football shouldn't feel like work

The game is beautiful. The feeds about it mostly aren't — every match a war, every teenager a failure by half-time.

Five-a-Side is the opposite machine. Three calm rooms, and then it ends. Nothing to pull down, nothing to refresh, nobody yelling.

Three rooms, one rule each

touchline
What happened. The league, identical for everyone who opens it. No squads, nothing personal.
the gaffers
What we did about it. Five squads, five weekly reads, one mini-league, and a roast that is only ever about a decision someone actually made.
the locker room
What Ted knows. Every player there is evidence on, the injury room and the fixture runs. A name in the file opens everything known about him.

Convictions baked in

The pulse, not the flood
Enough to hold your own at the pub — scores, the table, the week's real stories. If it doesn't help you love the game more, it doesn't make the page.
Facts from data, judgment marked as judgment
Prices, points, squads, chips and fixtures come from the data. Ted writes the opinions, labels them, and says what would change his mind.
A verdict is a hypothesis with a trigger
Four words — nailed, solid, watch, sack — each with one line of why and, written before the fact, what would change it. That last part makes a retro possible instead of a debate.
The long game
One weekend of upsets and stoppage-time luck is not evidence. Early in a season the page says so, and retires the warning once there is enough football to argue from.
Ted is the assistant manager
He watches everything, writes the verdicts and the weekly reads, and never picks the team. He is an AI and can be wrong; every verdict includes the trigger that would change his mind.
Nicknames only
Only nicknames appear here. Five-a-Side keeps the football separate from the names behind it.
Built to learn
Sibling of antifeed, and the same experiment: point an AI agent at an editorial job and let a human steer only taste.
